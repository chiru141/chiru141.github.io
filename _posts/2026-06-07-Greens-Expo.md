I pulled my brother to drive up to Ahmedabad for the Greens Expo this week. It is hosted as a key side event for World Economic Circular Forum which India is anchoring this year. 

The venue was buzzing with green-tech start-ups, recycling machinery, and a dense lineup of speaker sessions breaking down the latest Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) rules, carbon markets, and AI integration.

I went in looking to find people in the industry whose views expose the ground realities of the Indian recycling & policy space. 

By the time we headed back, it was clear that India's recycling and circularity industry is not limited by technology. It is bottlenecked by feedstock quality, unglamorous logistics, and structural economics.

<!--more-->

### 1. Observations from the Expo

Walking around the exhibition stalls, I was amazed at the various mechanical and chemical recycling technologies available to run commercially, recycling plastic, tyres, water and more.

The problem was helping the waste find its way to these machines. This led us to the buzzword of the expo: "AI-based sorting"
It's the next big challenge to solve.
Because while the recycling technology is more or less mature enough to be run commercially, availability for raw material which is segregated waste which can be run through these machines is not. 

While segregation is important and one of the most difficult problem to solve, One of the start-ups stood out. The start-up seemed to design around the complex sorting problem. It was the only start-up in the expo working in the Textile Industry. They didn't care about the fiber composition of the textile waste. They only need the fabrics sorted by color. They are converting textile waste to architectural sheets for walls and premium interiors.

### 2. Day 1 Speaker Sessions: Understanding Policy

The macro-policy landscape in India is shifting. The EPR frameworks are active, with platforms enabled by multiple modules including blockchain to ensure compliance.
The Emissions Trading Scheme (ETS) is unfolding with the National Carbon market scheduled to launch this year. 

As the speakers talked about the compliance architecture, I found myself questioning the need for end-of-life traceability. When a material is hitting its end-of-life, how would a digital trail really help? Brands often use QR codes in their sustainability brochures to claim 100% circularity at the point of sale, which essentially shifts the logistical burden onto the consumer. However, on the ground, these digital trails serve a different operational purpose: noting where the article's origins & its composition lie.

The consensus from the panel is that, EoL traceability would work as an anti-fraud mechanism. In an ecosystem where EPR certificates will carry real monetary value, a digital ledger would prevent double-counting of recycled materials. Furthermore, knowing the exact composition of an incoming component for recycling is important to avoid machine processing failures.

While the panel talked about the role of technology in the waste management space, Sandeep Patel from the NEPRA Group made a blunt point: the industry needs a holistic "Solution Approach" rather than an isolated tech approach. He goes on to say that he is not a big advocate of waste to energy approach because Indian municipal waste has an incredibly high moisture content and low calorific value; incinerating it is thermodynamically inefficient and permanently destroys resource value. He stresses that the industry should focus on recovery of resources.

He also thinks enforcement is a bigger driver than Incentive in India to drive momentum to the India's Circular Industry. 

Bottom line is that:
Waste is fundamentally an asset nobody wants to pay to manage. Until strict regulatory enforcement penalizes non-compliance, the economic incentives alone won’t balance the books. This is why Public-Private Partnerships (PPPs) are non-negotiable. The public sector must enforce source segregation and provide municipal land logistics, while the private sector brings the capital agility to upgrade the quality of recycled materials to near-virgin standards. 

### 3. Day 2 Speaker Sessions: E-waste Recycling Challenges & Upcoming Solar waste wave

Day 2 moved the conversations deeper into high-value, highly-complex domains of E-waste, Metals and Solar Waste management.

In metals and e-waste recycling, the monetary value is undeniably there but it resides in recovery of precious metals (Copper, silver, gold, etc), not in crude crushing or recycling of chassis. 

While India has a robust industry recycling E-waste, 80-90% of it is unorganized, leaving the organized sector with limited feedstock for recycling.

The panel highlighted the challenges of finding E-waste feedstock domestically and reliance on imports to keep their recycling plants running. I think that the informal sector is incredibly efficient at sorting, segregating, and collecting metal and e-waste in India. Rather than trying to dismantle this grassroots network, policy should focus on providing them with safety tools, financial inclusion, and formal integration. In fact, speakers admitted that the current economics of metal recycling only truly make sense within the lean operations of the unorganized space; the formal sector will require massive policy reforms just to stay financially viable.

Compounding the problem in E-waste recycling is, India's distinct capability deficit. We have a severe shortage of metallurgists and mining process experts who understand deep metallurgical extraction and can rigorously test the quality of recycled versus virgin materials. I am not sure if we aren't paying these experts enough, or have not signaled enough industrial demand to build that talent pipeline.

The final frontier is solar waste management, as the wave of early-2010s solar installations reach their end-of-life, the industry is only bound to expand. 

Until recently, interstate logistics for expired solar panels was a financial nightmare because panels were classified as hazardous waste due to trace heavy metals. The regulatory environment has recently pivoted: solar waste is now governed under separate solar waste guidelines, opening up cheaper, normalized logistics.

But this raises a critical question: Are solar panels actually safe now, or are we overlooking severe environmental risks just to make recycling commercially viable? The material reality is that panels still contain toxic heavy metals like lead and cadmium. If left in open landfills, they leach into groundwater. The new CPCB guidelines handle this via a deliberate trade-off: they use a dual-status approach where transport is handled under cheaper e-waste logistics to prevent unmonitored dumping, but strict, non-leachable, impervious flooring protocols remain mandatory for actual storage and final processing to prevent environmental leaching.

This logistical shift makes the proposition of Shyam Rungta from Regain Energies highly compelling: deploying decentralized micro-recycling plants (minimum 2 MW capacity) operating right on-site.

At first glance, I wondered if on-site modular plants make sense for an asset that is supposed to last 20 to 25 years. 
On a bit of reading, it was clear to me that the industrial reality of solar is defined by weather damage, micro-cracks, early component failures, and "repowering" where developers pull out older, lower-efficiency panels early to substitute them with modern high-output modules. For a large-scale solar park tracking hundreds of megawatts, solar waste isn't a distant 20-year milestone; it's it's a continuous, operational volume.

Instead of paying massive freight costs to ship heavy, low-density glass panels across the country to a central facility, deploying a 2 MW micro-recycling asset directly at the generation hub allows operators to strip the aluminum frames, harvest the glass aggregates, and concentrate the silver-copper trace components right at the source.

As my brother and I drove back, I kept returning to the ultimate question: Is the circular economy a genuine monetary opportunity today, or an Industry being driven by environmentalists, and government mandates & subsidies?

The pieces of the puzzle are floating around. The recycling tech is maturing day by day. The policy winds (EPR, ETS, Carbon Prices) are blowing in the right direction. But the monetization engine is stuck in first gear because of two massive frictions: erratic raw material supply chains and subpar output quality control (especially in direct competition with easily accessible cheaper virgin materials).

We will have to be a master of unglamorous reverse logistics, a shrewd negotiator of public partnerships, and a deep-tech processor capable of turning unpredictable feedstock into consistent, high-grade materials to win in this space.

India has spent the last decade perfecting hyper-efficient, last-mile commercial logistics for platforms like Meesho to reach every corner of the country. I wonder if we could leverage that same logistics infrastructure and reverse the flow and unlock the monetary scale of the circular economy.
