As part of the GCPP course at Takshashila, I attended a virtual Academic Conference over the weekend. The sessions spanned four core themes: Geopolitics, Geoeconomics, Technology, and Domestic Reforms.

With multiple sessions running in parallel, I found myself switching between sessions to catch a flavor of each. We debated global power shifts, the potential relative decline of the West, the ascent of China, India's manufacturing woes, Vietnam's rapid rise, and more.

While I can't capture every insight in a single post, four key themes took center stage:

1. **The US and the recent weaponization of trade policy** (targeted trade tariffs) to advance their national interests: While traditional economic theory treats international trade as a mutually beneficial win-win situation, recent developments have uncovered how economic tools can be used to advance national security and strategic interests. This indicates how US is actively trying to prevent its decline relative to China.

2. **Comparison between India and China, and how China was able to do what India couldn't**: Once looked upon as a cheap labor market, China evolved into a tech superpower producing deep-tech startups and global supply chain dominance. China expanded its economic capability to become a major superpower, growing its GDP by over 120 times[1] in the last 50 years while India's grew by around 38 times[2]. what did India did or failed to do to match China's growth trajectory came up in most discussions. Interestingly, one panel highlighted that China's shareholder market returns were -13%, compared to India's market returns of 1,300%, highlighting divergent priorities between the two countries. China prioritized state capacity, gross industrial output, and massive import-led manufacturing scale over corporate profitability and equity returns.

3. **Leveraging framework for modern public policy from Kautilya's Arthasashtra**: I particularly liked the concept of Yogashema, the dual mandate of Yoga (acquisition/security) and Kshema (preservation/welfare). In classical Indian statecraft, National Security and Social Welfare were not competing priorities, they were co-dependent. A state cannot fund robust defense without an expanding economic welfare base, nor can social welfare survive without strong national security mechanisms.

4. **An almost philosophical statement by one of the speakers**: *"There are no poor people, only people in poor places"* Individual productivity and economic mobility are heavily dictated by the local ecosystem, agglomeration of industries, and spatial connectivity. If the ecosystem lacks capital density, infrastructure, and institutional quality, individual effort hits a low ceiling. Solving poverty isn't just about cash transfers; it's about enabling geographic mobility or building productive spatial clusters.

While above were my key takeaways and direction of thoughts after the conference, one key question from the debates continues to linger in my mind: **Does it still make sense for India to aggressively pursue a manufacturing-led growth model, or has that window closed in an era of automation and AI?**

Based on my understanding, Manufacturing remains the only sector capable of absorbing millions of low- and mid-skilled workers transitioning out of agriculture, creating high employment multipliers in logistics, construction, and domestic supply chains. The Indian government is pushing for it, and believes the same.

I was surprised to hear the counter-argument.

The panel contended that as factories become increasingly automated through robotics and AI, pouring manufacturing capital in form of subsidies and otherwise no longer translates into mass low-skill job creation. Furthermore, labor market preferences are shifting away from manual factory work towards services due to concerns regarding working conditions, lack of social status, and quality lifestyle. Subsidizing basic assembly today may yield diminishing returns compared to investing in human capital and high-end services.

This paradox, of trying to fuel job creation while the labor markets dry up and factory floors automate seems to be the central challenge of modern Indian economic policy.

In my next piece, I will dive deeper into whether India should double down on advanced manufacturing clusters or forge an entirely distinct, service  and technology-led development path.
